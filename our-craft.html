Thirty-Two Stitches, One Tradition

Chikankari — literally "embroidery" in Persian — took root in the courts of Awadh in the late 18th century and has been worked by hand in and around Lucknow ever since. As the story is often told, it was introduced to the region by Nur Jahan, wife of the Mughal emperor Jahangir, who was captivated by Persian shadow-work embroidery; whatever its exact origin, it survived the fall of the Mughal court under Nawabi patronage and, later, as the livelihood of thousands of home-based artisans across Lucknow's villages.

What makes chikankari unlike any other embroidery tradition in India is the sheer range of stitches used to build a single motif — traditionally said to number 32, worked in combination across four broad families: flat stitches, raised and embossed stitches, open trellis or jaali work, and decorative finishing stitches. Every Dastkari Chikan piece draws on this same vocabulary.

The Process

How a Piece Is Made

Long before a needle touches the fabric, a design is chosen and hand-carved wooden blocks stamp its outline onto the cloth in washable ink. Only then does the embroidery begin.

01

Block-Printed Guideline

A hand-carved wooden block stamps the motif outline onto plain cotton, muslin or georgette in a temporary, washable dye — the artisan's map for everything that follows.

02

Hand Embroidery

Working entirely by hand — often from the reverse side of the cloth — an artisan combines several of chikankari's 32 stitches to build a single motif, a process that can take days for one garment.

03

Wash & Finish

The finished piece is washed to remove the printed guideline entirely, then starched and pressed — revealing embroidery that looks as clean from the front as it does from the back.

Family One

Flat Stitches

Worked close to the surface of the fabric, flat stitches merge so completely with the cloth that they read less like embroidery and more like the fabric's own texture. Most chikankari motifs begin with one of these as an outline.

01

Tepchi

A long running stitch worked on the right side of the fabric, catching four threads and picking up one. It forms the foundational line that most other stitches are built upon.

02

Bakhiya

Chikankari's signature "shadow work," worked entirely from the reverse in a herringbone path so the thread casts a soft shadow through to the right side. Comes as Ulti Bakhiya (open, translucent) or Sidhi Bakhiya (closed, opaque).

03

Khatau

A finer, appliqué-like cousin of bakhiya: a motif is embroidered separately, then laid over and stitched onto the final fabric.

04

Pechni

A tightly twisted stitch used to outline motifs with a subtly coiled texture, often paired with bakhiya for definition.

05

Rahet

A solid double-back stitch, most often doubled as "dohra bakhiya" to outline a motif with a firm, continuous line.

06

Banjkali

An interlocking, twisted stitch used along borders and motif edges for a woven, textured finish.

07

Keel Kangan

A stem-stitch variant used to outline stalks, vines, and bangle-shaped motif borders.

08

Ghas Patti

"Grass leaf" — graduated, V-shaped stitches worked in rows to fill petals and leaves like blades of grass.

Family Two

Raised & Embossed Stitches

Where flat stitches recede into the cloth, this family sits proudly above it — tiny knots and eyelets that give chikankari its signature dimension and texture, most often found at the heart of a floral motif.

09

Murri

Diagonal satin stitches knotted over a tepchi base to form a tiny rice-grain shape — among the oldest and most prized of all chikankari stitches.

10

Phanda

A millet-shaped knot, smaller and more delicate than murri, most often clustered at the centre of a flower motif.

11

Dhum (Gol Murri)

A rounder, fuller knot stitch used as a variation on murri for denser floral centres.

12

Muthra

Tiny pearl-like stitches that add a beaded texture along borders and motif edges.

13

Zanzeera

A fine chain stitch used to give leaf and petal outlines a crisp, finished edge once the main embroidery is complete.

14

Hool

A fine detached eyelet — a small hole is punched in the fabric and the threads teased apart, then rimmed in satin stitch to form a flower's centre.

15

Bijli

"Lightning" — a dynamic zigzag stitch used for borders and accents that mimic a lightning bolt's path.

16

Kapkapi

A quivering, closely-set stitch that catches the light for a subtly shimmering texture.

Family Three

Jaali — Open Trellis Work

Chikankari's most technically demanding family, and the one found nowhere else in Indian embroidery: the fabric's own warp and weft threads are teased apart and secured with minute buttonhole stitches, opening a net-like lattice without ever piercing the front of the cloth.

17

Jaali

The classic trellis stitch — threads are drawn apart, never pierced from the front, so the reverse looks as clean as the face of the fabric.

18

Bulbul Chashm

"Nightingale's eye" — a delicate, rounded variation of jaali worked in small, tight circles.

19

Madrazi

A denser, more closely-worked trellis variation, giving a firmer net-like texture than classic jaali.

20

Sidhaul Jaali

A straight-line variation of trellis work, used to build geometric fill patterns within a motif.

21

Hathkadi

A straight-line jaali worked in narrow bands, often used along borders and cuffs.

22

Bank Jaali

An angled variation of trellis work, opened on the diagonal for a woven, basket-like look.

23

Phool Jaali

A floral variation of trellis work, opened within the exact shape of a flower or petal outline.

24

Taj Mahal

An elaborate, architecturally-inspired trellis pattern, named for its intricate, monument-like symmetry.

Family Four

Decorative & Finishing Stitches

The stitches that give a finished piece its polish — hemming, seam-work, and small motif details layered in once the primary embroidery is complete.

25

Turpai

A fine hemming stitch that finishes raw edges with the appearance of a single, unbroken thread.

26

Darzdari

A family of decorative seam stitches — including Kohi Darz, Kamal Darz, Shankarpara Darz, Muchii and Singhbhada Darz — used to finish and embellish seams.

27

Chanapatti

"Gram leaf" — a small, rounded leaf-shaped fill stitch worked in rows, tighter and fuller than ghas patti.

28

Dhania Patti

"Coriander leaf" — a fine leaf-shaped stitch used for delicate borders and trailing vine motifs.

29

Rozan

A rose-inspired decorative stitch worked in small clustered florets.

30

Meharki

A combination stitch layering two or more techniques together for a richly detailed, ornamental finish.

31

Baalda

A dense, textured combination stitch used to add weight and detail to statement motifs.

32

Jora

A paired stitch, worked in twos, used to finish and reinforce borders across garments and home linen alike.
